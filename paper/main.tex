\begin{abstract}
Structured-light illumination (SLI) assumes that the camera integrates exactly one projected
pattern per exposure. With an off-the-shelf digital light processing (DLP) projector that
assumption is rarely checked: the projector re-times the video it receives, delays it by an
unknown latency, and paints each frame as an interleaved sequence of colour-LED and bit-plane
pulses whose timing is undocumented. We present a ``bring your own projector'' (BYOP) camera in
which a field-programmable gate array sits inline on the HDMI cable between a PC and an
unmodified projector. The same device reads the projector's EDID and drives it in a mode it
advertises, generates resolution-adaptive phase-shift patterns on the fly, triggers a
global-shutter PYTHON~1300 sensor at a programmable delay from the projected vertical sync with
\SI{10}{\nano\second} resolution, and queues triggers so that patterns advance at the full video
rate even when the delay exceeds a frame. Because the device controls both the picture and the
shutter, it can also measure the projector: stepping a \SI{4.875}{\micro\second} exposure across
a five-frame impulse sequence recovers the light the projector emits against time, which we
use to place the exposure, to decompose white into its red, green and blue LED pulses, and to
map the light that leaks into an exposure from the neighbouring patterns. On an Optoma ML750ST
at \SI{120}{\hertz} we find a one-frame latency and a train of separate red, green and blue LED pulses whose
filtered decomposition explains white with $R^2=0.9988$, and an exposure window that captures
\SI{95.6}{\percent} of a frame's light (\SI{99.2}{\percent} at the sensor's longest exposure);
light before the window depends on the previous pattern in one part and on the next pattern in
another. \pending{3D results: how much profiling and leakage correction improve phase and depth
error.}
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Structured light, phase measuring profilometry, projector--camera systems, DLP, genlock,
FPGA, temporal characterization.
\end{IEEEkeywords}}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

% ---- 1. What structured light is, and how it differs from other 3D imaging ----------
\IEEEPARstart{S}{tructured} light illumination (SLI) refers to a method of 3D scanning that
uses a projector to project a series of light striped patterns such that a camera can
reconstruct depth based on the warping of the patterns over the target object's
surface~\cite{salvi2004pattern,geng2011tutorial,zuo2018phase}. In this way, the projector takes
the place of the second camera in a stereo pair, and the correspondence between a camera pixel
and a projector row or column is established by the projected patterns rather than by the
texture of the scene. Passive stereo vision, by comparison, must find correspondences from
scene texture and fails on surfaces that have none~\cite{scharstein2002taxonomy}, while
time-of-flight (ToF) cameras measure depth directly from the round-trip time of modulated light
but at a lower lateral resolution and with depth errors caused by multi-path
reflections~\cite{hansard2012tof}. Laser line scanners, also based on triangulation, measure a
single profile per captured image~\cite{blais2004review}.

% ---- 2. It is a scanning process: motion is the enemy, so scan fast --------------------
Examples of SLI include single pattern techniques which project a static pattern from which a 3D
reconstruction can be made from a single snapshot~\cite{takeda1983fourier,geng2011tutorial}.
Multiple pattern SLI scanners, alternatively, project a series of patterns, trading temporal
resolution for spatial resolution such that each pixel can be independently processed from its
neighbors to produce a single point for each pixel in the camera. In phase measuring
profilometry (PMP), the projector coordinate of each pixel is encoded through phase modulation
over a minimum of three patterns, with multi-frequency schemes requiring
more~\cite{liu2010dualfreq,zuo2018phase}. As a scanning process, multiple pattern SLI assumes
that the target object is stationary for the duration of the scan; when the object moves, the
samples recorded at a pixel come from different surface points, and the reconstruction suffers
from ripple and decoding errors~\cite{weise2007fast,zhang2018review}. For this reason, it is
desirable for the pattern sequence to run as fast as possible.

% ---- 3+4. Cameras cap the useful rate; light engines vs commodity projectors -----------
In practice, three factors limit the scan rate: the frame rate of the camera, the light output of
the projector, and the bandwidth between the camera and the host computer. Each pattern must be
captured in its own image, and machine vision cameras with megapixel global-shutter sensors
operate at around \SI{200}{fps}; the PYTHON~1300 sensor used in this paper operates at up to
\SI{210}{fps} at full resolution. DLP light engines built for structured light display binary
patterns at tens of thousands of patterns per second, a rate that such cameras cannot use, and
they are either expensive or have low lumen counts (Section~\ref{sec:rw_engines}). A projector
with a low lumen count requires a longer exposure to collect the same signal, and since each
exposure must end before the next pattern's exposure begins, the exposure time sets a lower bound
on the time per pattern. Every image must also be transferred to the host: a $1280\times1024$
image at 10 bits per pixel is \SI{1.64}{MB}, so \SI{120}{fps} requires \SI{197}{MB/s} of
sustained bandwidth between the camera and the host.

For these reasons, commodity projectors are an attractive alternative. ``3D-ready'' DLP
projectors accept \SI{120}{\hertz} video and can emit more than 1500 lumens, and if a light
output below 1000 lumens is acceptable, then LED projectors can be used, whose LED lifetimes of
tens of thousands of hours make them practical for industrial applications
(Section~\ref{sec:rw_engines}).
% ---- 5. Commodity projectors are hard to set up: no stored patterns, no GPIO -----------
Commodity projectors are, however, difficult to integrate into a 3D scanner. A commodity
projector cannot store a static set of patterns in hardware, so every pattern must be sent over
the video cable by a host computer whose graphics output makes no guarantee as to when each
frame is delivered. A commodity projector also cannot be slaved to a camera acting as master
through general purpose input/output (GPIO), nor can it trigger a camera, as structured light
light engines do~\cite{barreto2011dlp}. Of the published scanners built on commodity projectors
(Section~\ref{sec:rw_sync}), only that of Yu
\emph{et~al.}~\cite{yu2019dualspie,yu2020dualprojector} uses a controller fully interfaced between
the HDMI video signal and the GPIO trigger of the camera.

% ---- 6. The BYOP camera (from the press release) ---------------------------------------
The \emph{bring your own projector} (BYOP) machine vision camera was developed to control an
unmodified projector through its HDMI input~\cite{lau2026byop}, and it follows from the
structured light smart camera for spatial augmented reality applications of Ruffner
\emph{et~al.}~\cite{ruffner2019smartcamera}. The BYOP camera is a
global-shutter machine vision camera, streaming full frames over USB~3.0, with an HDMI receiver
and transmitter integrated on the same FPGA. Video from a host computer enters the camera and
passes through to the projector, so the camera sits in the video path rather than beside it and
knows what is being projected and when.

When a host computer drives the display, the camera
either passes the host's pixels through untouched and, using the top-left pixel method of
Yu~\cite{yu2019dissertation}, triggers only when the top-left pixel of the incoming
frame changes, or
replaces the host's pixels with structured light patterns of its own. When no host is attached,
the camera reads the projector's EDID, drives the projector at a mode the projector advertises,
generates its own pattern sequence, and triggers the sensor at a programmable delay after each
vertical sync it transmits, at up to \SI{120}{\hertz}. No trigger box, frame grabber, or
synchronization cabling is needed, and because the HDMI path runs through the camera, multiple
BYOP cameras can be daisy-chained on a single projector.

% ---- 7. Commodity projectors are designed for viewers, which causes problems for SLI ---
Controlling when a frame is sent to the projector does not, by itself, control when or how the
projector emits the light of that frame. Commodity projectors are designed to produce a
compelling display for a human viewer watching movies, and the processing between the HDMI input
and the emitted light serves that purpose rather than measurement. That processing delays each
frame by an input lag that depends on the display mode, renders a constant input level as a
temporal sequence of mirror states, applies a nonlinear tone curve, and can redistribute the RGB
input among illumination components that the HDMI signal cannot address independently
(Section~\ref{sec:rw_electronics}).

An RGB LED projector also creates a problem of its own for SLI. A structured light pattern sent
as gray levels, with equal red, green, and blue values, is projected as the sum of three
patterns, one from each LED, and a pixel of a monochrome camera records each of them
weighted by the reflectance of the surface in that LED's band, so that a green surface returns
mostly the green pattern and a blue surface mostly the blue. If the three LEDs produce the same
phase, the recorded phase is independent of the surface color. If they do not, because the LEDs
have different tone curves or because the exposure collects a different share of each LED's
light, then each pixel records a mixture of phases whose balance depends on its color, which is
the same form as the multi-path interference studied by Zhang
\emph{et~al.}~\cite{zhang2019bimodal,zhang2024bimodalps}, and the error cannot be removed by a
calibration made under the combined RGB light. Each LED must therefore be, by itself, a correct
SLI projector that produces the same phase as the other two.

Projecting through only one LED does not avoid this requirement. Restricting the patterns to a
subset of the red, green, and blue channels discards the light of the other LEDs and so reduces
the light output of the projector, which is the property for which a commodity projector is
chosen. A pattern sent as pure red, green, or blue also need not be displayed by one LED alone:
the projector's color processing maps each input color to the drive of its light sources, in the
manner of an ICC color profile, and can turn on more than one LED to display a single
primary~\cite{stone2001color,ti_dlpa038} (Section~\ref{sec:rw_electronics}).

% ---- 8. What this paper does -----------------------------------------------------------
This being the case, this paper studies the profiling of an LED commodity projector through the
BYOP camera, with the aim of compensating the HDMI signal so as to minimize the distortion caused
by the projector's electronics while achieving a \SI{120}{\hertz} scan rate.

\subsection{Contributions}
This paper is the first to describe the BYOP camera, and so the first contribution is the camera
itself, implemented on a single Xilinx Artix-7 FPGA. On the video side, the FPGA includes an HDMI
receiver that recovers the pixel clock of the incoming video by measuring its frequency and
selecting the matching clock-recovery band, such that it tracks the spread-spectrum clocks of
graphics cards, and an HDMI transmitter that either forwards the host's video or replaces it with
patterns generated on the FPGA. The FPGA reads the projector's EDID over the display data channel
and serves the host a merged EDID listing only the modes that both the projector and the camera
support.

With no host attached, the FPGA reprograms its own pixel clock at run time and selects,
from a table of 14 video modes, the fastest mode that the projector's EDID advertises, up to
1280$\times$1024 at \SI{60}{\hertz} or 800$\times$600 at \SI{120}{\hertz}. The on-board pattern
generator produces PMP patterns at three spatial frequencies in a fixed $1{:}6{:}36$ ratio with
eight phase shifts each, plus a flat full-field pattern, with the periods recomputed for the
resolution of each video mode, and passes every pattern through a 256-entry lookup table that
can be loaded from the host.

On the camera side, the FPGA triggers the PYTHON~1300 sensor at a
delay after the vertical sync of the video it transmits, programmable in \SI{10}{\nano\second}
steps. In the controller of Yu \emph{et~al.}~\cite{yu2020dualprojector}, the camera trigger
waited out the trigger delay and the exposure before the next frame could be handled, so the
fastest frame period was the trigger delay plus the exposure. The BYOP camera instead holds
triggers in a 32-entry queue of absolute fire times, stacking a new trigger at every vertical
sync while earlier ones are still pending, so that the frame period is limited only by the
exposure and a delay longer than one frame period still yields one image per projected frame.

The FPGA
limits the exposure to the frame period less the sensor's readout gap, receives the sensor's four
LVDS lanes at \SI{720}{Mbps}, buffers frames in DDR3 memory, and streams them to the host over
USB~3.0. In pass-through mode, the FPGA
samples the top-left pixel of every incoming HDMI frame and triggers the camera when that value
changes, and it writes the sampled value into the header of the captured frame, so that the host
can match each captured image to the pattern that was displayed. The header also carries the
mean intensity of a $16\times16$ pixel region of interest computed on the FPGA, which allows the
projector profiling of this paper to run without transferring images.

While this paper limits its discussion to a single LED projector, the Optoma ML750ST, we note
that the profiling procedure requires nothing from the projector other than an HDMI input, and so
it can be applied to any projector that the BYOP camera can drive. Using the BYOP camera's own
sensor, we sweep a \SI{4.875}{\micro\second} exposure across a five-frame impulse sequence to
measure the light emitted by the projector against time, from which we derive the projector's
latency, the timing of its LED pulses, and the exposure window that collects the most light from a
single video frame.

By repeating the sweep through red, green, and blue filters, we test whether
the projector's white output contains any interval during which two LEDs are on at once, which
determines whether the three channels of the HDMI signal fully control the emitted light, and we
measure the timing of each LED's light separately, which is the first requirement for the three
LEDs to produce the same phase. \pending{Per-LED phase test: scan a flat target through each
filter and compare the three phase maps.}

We
further measure the light that reaches the camera before the exposure window opens, and by means
of two-dimensional maps over pairs of gray levels, separate the light arriving from the previous
pattern from the light arriving from the pattern being captured. \pending{Compensation of
the HDMI signal from these measurements, and its effect on phase and depth error.}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

This section reviews the patterns of SLI and the projection hardware that displays them, then the
ways that cameras have been synchronized with commodity projectors, the FPGA cameras that use the
same sensor as the BYOP camera, the processing inside a projector that stands between its video
input and its light, and finally multi-path, the class of errors to which that processing
contributes.

\subsection{Structured light and phase shifting}
Pattern codification strategies for SLI are surveyed by Salvi
\emph{et~al.}~\cite{salvi2004pattern} and by Geng~\cite{geng2011tutorial}, and phase-shifting
algorithms by Zuo \emph{et~al.}~\cite{zuo2018phase}. Liu \emph{et~al.}~\cite{liu2010dualfreq}
combine a fine and a coarse stripe pattern in each image so that fewer patterns are needed to
resolve which stripe a pixel sees, and Liu \emph{et~al.}~\cite{liu2010gamma} show that the
projector's tone curve, which makes the displayed brightness grow faster than the input value,
produces a repeating error in the measured phase. The pattern generator of the BYOP camera uses
three stripe frequencies in a fixed 1:6:36 ratio, and it passes every pattern through a lookup
table so that the projector's tone curve can be corrected before the patterns are sent.

High-speed SLI systems avoid the limits of video projection by displaying black-and-white
patterns directly on a DMD. Zhang and Huang~\cite{zhang2006realtime} as well as Zhang
\emph{et~al.}~\cite{zhang2010superfast} use this approach for real-time and superfast phase
shifting, and Lei and Zhang~\cite{lei2009defocus} defocus the projector so that the
black-and-white stripes blur into smooth gray-level stripes. Gong and
Zhang~\cite{gong2010ultrafast} apply the same defocusing with an off-the-shelf DLP projector, and
a review of these methods is given by Zhang~\cite{zhang2018review}. These systems depend on
projection hardware that exposes the DMD directly, which brings us to the light engines built
for that purpose.

\subsection{Light engines and commodity projectors}
\label{sec:rw_engines}
The DLP chipsets designed for high-speed pattern display operate far faster than any camera that
watches them. The DLPC410 controller drives the DLP7000 digital micromirror device (DMD) at up to
23\,000 black-and-white patterns per second over the full array, and at up to 48\,000 patterns
per second in a mode that repeats each row of the pattern four times and so gives up three
quarters of the vertical resolution~\cite{ti_dlpc410,ti_dlp7000}. Either rate exceeds the frame
rate of a megapixel machine vision camera by more than two orders of magnitude.

Barreto describes the structured light modes, stored pattern sequences, and trigger outputs of
TI's development kits~\cite{barreto2011dlp}. These kits and the industrial light engines built on
the same chipsets are either expensive or have low lumen counts. TI's DLP LightCrafter~4500, for
example, displays up to 4225 black-and-white patterns per second from an RGB LED light engine
rated at 150 lumens~\cite{ti_dlplcr4500evm}. At the other extreme, TI's DLP Discovery~4100
platform, which displays binary patterns at up to 32\,000 per second, consists of a controller
board listed at about US\$2400 and a DMD board listed at about US\$2200 for the
DLP7000~\cite{ti_dlpu040,mouser_d4100}, and these prices buy the mirrors and their electronics
only: the platform includes no light source, illumination optics, or projection lens, which must
be supplied separately~\cite{ti_dlpu040}.

Commodity projectors make the opposite trade, displaying patterns at a rate that machine vision
cameras can match. ``3D-ready'' DLP projectors, built for shutter-glass stereo viewing, accept
\SI{120}{\hertz} video and can have light outputs above 1500 lumens. As an example,
ProjectorCentral measured 2494 lumens from the 3D-ready BenQ W600 in its brightest mode, and 1748
lumens in its standard mode~\cite{pc_benq_w600}. The Optoma ML750ST used in this paper is an LED
DLP projector with individual red, green, and blue LEDs and a rated LED life of 20\,000 hours, and
it accepts 800$\times$600 video at \SI{120}{\hertz}~\cite{optoma_ml750st,pc_ml750st_specs}.

Optoma rates the ML750ST at 700 lumens, a figure that ProjectorCentral reports as the perceived
equivalent of a lamp projector's lumens rather than a measured light output; ProjectorCentral
measured 490 lumens in the ML750ST's brightest mode and 322 lumens in its cinema
mode~\cite{pc_ml750st_review}. ProjectorCentral also lists the ML750ST's input lag, the delay
between the arrival of a frame at the HDMI input and its appearance on screen, as
\SI{16.5}{\milli\second} for 1080p video at \SI{60}{\hertz}~\cite{pc_ml750st_specs}.

We further note that faster scan rates are available from commodity 4K DLP projectors. TI's
0.47-inch 4K UHD DMD has 1920$\times$1080 micromirrors and builds a 3840$\times$2160 image by
shifting the picture among four positions with an optical actuator, and the chipsets built on
this DMD accept 4K video at \SI{60}{\hertz} or 1080p video at
\SI{240}{\hertz}~\cite{ti_dlp471te,ti_dlpc6540}. An SLI scanner built around a commodity 4K DLP
projector could therefore scan at \SI{240}{\hertz}; such a scanner has not yet been demonstrated.
Whatever its rate, a commodity projector accepts patterns only through its video input and
offers no trigger, so a scanner built on one must synchronize its camera by other means.

\subsection{Synchronizing commodity projectors and cameras}
\label{sec:rw_sync}
Examples of how to deal with this lack of synchronization include Wilm
\emph{et~al.}~\cite{wilm2014calibration}, who insert fixed pauses between pattern presentation and
image capture to accommodate buffering in the graphics card and projector, as well as Petkovi\'c
\emph{et~al.}~\cite{petkovic2016software}, who use the blanking interval between video frames as
a timing reference and estimate the combined projector--camera delay from captured images. Moreno
\emph{et~al.}~\cite{moreno2015unsync} take the approach of running projector and camera
independently and computing, from the unsynchronized images, the images that a synchronized
system would have captured. Zuo \emph{et~al.}~\cite{zuo2012highspeed}, alternatively, modify a
commodity projector with custom hardware that drives its DMD directly.

While each of these systems uses a commodity projector, none of them solves the problem of
triggering the camera from the projector. To the best of our knowledge, Yu
\emph{et~al.}~\cite{yu2019dualspie,yu2020dualprojector} are the first and only to demonstrate an
SLI scanner with a controller fully interfaced between the HDMI video signal and the GPIO trigger
of the camera.

Their controller operates either in an offline mode, in which it generates the video and patterns
itself, or in a pass-through mode, in which it forwards video from a host computer; for their
dual-projector scanner, both of the controller's HDMI ports were used as outputs, one to each
projector~\cite{yu2020dualprojector}. As an example of a feature of the pass-through mode,
Yu~\cite{yu2019dissertation} describes reading the top-left pixel of each incoming frame and
triggering the camera only when that pixel's value changes, so that frames repeated by the host's
graphics processing unit (GPU) are not captured. Their controller waits out the trigger delay and
the exposure before handling the next frame, a limitation analyzed in
Section~\ref{sec:stacking}. That limitation follows from controlling an external camera through
GPIO, and removing it means building the camera into the FPGA itself.

\subsection{FPGA cameras built on the PYTHON 1300}
Building the camera into the FPGA requires the FPGA to configure the image sensor itself, and the
configuration of most image sensors is not public. The onsemi PYTHON~1300 is a 1.3-megapixel
global-shutter CMOS sensor that reads out at up to \SI{210}{fps} at its full 1280$\times$1024
resolution over four LVDS data lanes, and it is configured over a serial peripheral interface
(SPI). Its datasheet lists the sensor's registers, but not the values that make the sensor
stream: the registers of the clock generator, image core, analog front end, bias, and LVDS
outputs are listed only as ``configure according to recommendation,'' the reserved registers must
be loaded with values that are not given, and the datasheet states that the register uploads for
each operating mode are available to customers under a non-disclosure agreement
(NDA)~\cite{onsemi_python1300}. An FPGA design that is to be published therefore cannot be written
from the datasheet alone.

For this reason, we chose a sensor for which a working configuration had already been published
with an FPGA design. Three such designs connect the PYTHON~1300 directly to an FPGA. Avnet
publishes a reference design for its PYTHON-1300-C camera module, with files for several Zynq
development boards, an SPI controller for the sensor, and the complete register upload sequence,
including the sensor's timing program~\cite{avnet_hdl}. The Open Vision Computer of Quigley
\emph{et~al.}~\cite{quigley2019ovc} is an embedded vision system for mobile robots: its first
revision carries three PYTHON~1300 sensors on an Artix-7 FPGA with DRAM and a USB~3 controller,
and later revisions pair two PYTHON~1300 sensors with an Intel Cyclone FPGA and an NVIDIA Jetson
TX2~\cite{osrf_ovc}.

Chalich \emph{et~al.}~\cite{chalich2020lowcost} built a low-cost, standalone high-speed camera
around the monochrome PYTHON~1300 and a MicroZed board with a Zynq-7020, reaching \SI{211}{fps}
at full resolution and \SI{2329}{fps} at 256$\times$256, with frames stored in DDR3 memory and
saved to an SD card, and they release the design as open source~\cite{hfrc}. Each of these designs
reads the sensor and stores or streams its images; none of them has a video interface or
synchronizes the sensor to a projector. The BYOP camera's receive path was written for this work,
using the Avnet register sequence and the pixel ordering of the Open Vision Computer's decoder as
references.

\subsection{Projector electronics}
\label{sec:rw_electronics}
With the camera inside the video path, the remaining question is how the projector turns that
video into light, since its electronics stand between the frame the camera knows about and the
light the camera receives. Petkovi\'c \emph{et~al.}~\cite{petkovic2016software} note that
triggering a camera from the video signal ignores the projector's input lag, which they measured
at \SI{16.8}{\milli\second} in one projector's \SI{120}{\hertz} 3D mode and 30--\SI{42}{\milli\second}
in its other modes. Narasimhan \emph{et~al.}~\cite{narasimhan2008dithering} as well as Koppal
\emph{et~al.}~\cite{koppal2012dlp} show that a DLP projector displays a constant gray level as a
rapid sequence of mirror flips between on and off, such that the light collected by a short
exposure depends on when that exposure occurs within the frame.

The projector also changes what is displayed, not only when. Stone~\cite{stone2001color}
describes the tone curves that projector electronics apply to the input, and she, as well as
Seime and Hardeberg~\cite{seime2003colorimetric} and Wyble and Zhang~\cite{wyble2003dlp}, show that
DLP projectors with a clear segment in their color wheel convert the input red, green, and blue
values into red, green, blue, and white light.

Cotting \emph{et~al.}~\cite{cotting2004embedding} measured the consequence directly, finding that
for some pixel values a small change in one color changes the mirror sequence of the other
colors; they measured these sequences first with a phototransistor and then with a camera whose
exposure start time was swept across the frame. LED projectors eliminate the color wheel but not
necessarily the problem, since DLP controllers can turn on two or more LEDs at once to produce
yellow, cyan, magenta, or white intervals~\cite{ti_dlpa038}, light that cannot be controlled
independently through the three color channels of an HDMI signal.

\subsection{Light transport and multi-path}
Light that reaches a camera pixel from more than one source is a familiar problem in SLI,
although it is usually studied in space rather than in time. As an active imaging technique,
structured light is susceptible to errors caused by the redirection of the projected light to
form multiple paths from projector to target to camera besides the direct path, and the same
problem is found in other 3D imaging modalities such as time-of-flight (ToF), where light reflects
off specular surfaces onto neighboring surface points before returning to the camera.

Examples of how to deal with multi-path in ToF include Dorrington
\emph{et~al.}~\cite{dorrington2011separating} as well as Bhandari
\emph{et~al.}~\cite{bhandari2013multifrequency} and Godbaz \emph{et~al.}~\cite{godbaz2012closed},
who take the common approach of measuring depth at many modulation frequencies and fitting the
strength and delay of each path. Freedman \emph{et~al.}~\cite{freedman2014sra} assume that only a
few paths contribute, Naik \emph{et~al.}~\cite{naik2015light} combine ToF measurements with a model
of direct and indirect light, and Kadambi \emph{et~al.}~\cite{kadambi2013coded}, O'Toole
\emph{et~al.}~\cite{otoole2014temporal}, and Gupta \emph{et~al.}~\cite{gupta2015phasor} code the
illumination in time to separate the paths.

In SLI, Nayar \emph{et~al.}~\cite{nayar2006separation} separate the light that travels directly
from the projector to a surface point from the light that arrives indirectly, using finely
striped illumination, Gupta \emph{et~al.}~\cite{gupta2011global} design patterns that are robust
to indirect light, and Gupta and Nayar~\cite{gupta2012micro} restrict phase shifting to a narrow
band of fine stripe patterns. Chen \emph{et~al.}~\cite{chen2008modulated} modulate the
phase-shifting patterns to reject light scattered beneath the surface, Couture
\emph{et~al.}~\cite{couture2011unstructured} replace striped patterns with random ones to
overcome interreflections, and Dedrick~\cite{dedrick2011sli} studies SLI in optically
challenging environments. O'Toole
\emph{et~al.}~\cite{otoole2012primaldual,otoole2014slt,otoole2015homogeneous} synchronize the
camera's exposure to the projector to select or reject particular light paths.

Zhang \emph{et~al.}~\cite{zhang2019bimodal,zhang2024bimodalps} address the case in which a camera
pixel receives light from exactly two points on the projector, such as along a step edge, and
separate the two by measuring how the two stripe signals reinforce and cancel as the stripe
frequency changes. An RGB LED projector viewed by a monochrome camera produces a mixture of the
same kind without any second surface. Each pixel records the sum of three stripe patterns, one
per LED, each scaled by how strongly the surface reflects that LED's color. If the three LEDs
place their stripes at the same position, the surface color changes only the contrast of the
recorded stripes, and the measured phase is correct. If they do not, the measured phase is pulled
toward the LED that the surface reflects most, so the same shape measures differently in
different colors. Light from a neighboring pattern that falls inside the exposure, which this
paper measures, adds a further contribution of the same kind, separated from the intended pattern
in time rather than in space.

The three LEDs can also place their stripes at different positions because of the projection
lens. Zhang, Towers, and Towers~\cite{zhang2010lateral} show that lateral chromatic aberration
gives the red, green, and blue channels of a color fringe projection system different phase maps,
and they correct it from the difference between channels. Huang
\emph{et~al.}~\cite{huang2016lateral} compare the shift between color channels seen on printed
targets, which comes only from the camera's lens, against the shift seen on projected targets,
which comes from both lenses, and find that the projector dominates in their DLP system, with a
shift that changes with the distance to the target. Zhang \emph{et~al.}~\cite{zhang2022chromatic}
separate the projector's shift from the camera's with an LCD phase target and attribute it to the
projection lens: although every color is formed by the same DMD mirrors, the lens images each
color at a slightly different position, by up to 1.96~pixels in their DLP projector.

How strongly each LED contributes is set by the target. Wang
\emph{et~al.}~\cite{wang2021nonuniform} project red, green, and blue stripes with a DLP light
engine onto colored surfaces viewed by a monochrome camera, observe that each colored region
returns strong stripes only under the LED of its own color, and adapt the projected color and
exposure to the surface; they attribute the wrinkles that remain in their reconstructions to the
projector's tone curve. Zhu \emph{et~al.}~\cite{zhu2022colorful} calibrate the projector's
brightness response in advance to measure colorful objects.

The same dependence on surface color limits what a brightness calibration on a white target can
establish. Projecting a series of gray levels onto a white target and recording them with a
monochrome camera measures the sum of the three LEDs' responses, and a lookup table built from
that measurement straightens only the sum. If the three LEDs have tone curves of different
shapes, one LED's curvature can cancel another's on the white target and then return on a colored
target that reflects one LED more than the others, distorting the recorded stripes. A single
correction works for every target only when the three LEDs' tone curves have the same shape.

The BYOP camera is a stack of four functional circuit boards and two spacers, joined by
Alchitry's board-to-board connectors, with no internal cables. Three of the functional boards are
commercially available. The Alchitry Pt~V2 carries a Xilinx Artix-7 XC7A100T FPGA, \SI{256}{MB}
of DDR3L memory, a \SI{100}{\mega\hertz} oscillator, and 206 I/O pins~\cite{alchitry_pt}. Below it,
the Alchitry Ft+~V2 provides a USB~3.0 link through an FTDI FT601Q bridge with a 32-bit parallel
FIFO interface to the FPGA~\cite{alchitry_ftplus,ftdi_ft601}, and below that, the Alchitry Hd~V2
provides two HDMI ports, each configurable as an input or an output, which carry the video from
the host computer and to the projector~\cite{alchitry_hd}. One spacer between the Pt~V2 and the
Ft+~V2 clears the capacitors on the underside of the Pt~V2, and a second between the Ft+~V2 and
the Hd~V2 is needed because, without it, the Ft+~V2's USB connector blocks the cable of the
Hd~V2's transmitting HDMI port~\cite{alchitry_pt,alchitry_hd}.

The three boards must share the Pt~V2's I/O pins without conflict, and each FPGA bank has a
single I/O supply voltage. On the Pt~V2, banks 14, 16, 34, and 35 are fixed at \SI{3.3}{V},
bank~15 runs at \SI{1.35}{V} for the on-board DDR3L, and bank~13 is the only bank whose voltage
can be switched, between \SI{3.3}{V}, \SI{2.5}{V}, and
\SI{1.8}{V}~\cite{alchitry_pt_sch,alchitry_labs}. The Hd~V2 uses pins A45--A78 of the bottom
connectors, at \SI{3.3}{V}, for its TMDS pairs and its DDC, CEC, and hot-plug lines, and the
Ft+~V2 uses pins A3--A42 and B3--B24, also at \SI{3.3}{V}, for the FT601's data bus and control
lines; the two sets are disjoint, so these boards conflict only mechanically. The sensor's LVDS
lanes require \SI{2.5}{V}, and the only pins that can run at that voltage are the 32 pins of
bank~13, which appear only on the top connector, as pins B33--B78. Neither the Hd~V2 nor the
Ft+~V2 uses any of them, so the sensor card takes all 32, which is why it sits on top of the
stack. For the same reason, the Alchitry Fn board cannot take the place of a spacer, because one
of its solder jumpers lands on pin B36 and would take half of one LVDS pair.

An off-the-shelf alternative would combine an FPGA board that has HDMI ports with a camera module.
Avnet's MicroZed Embedded Vision Kit, for example, pairs a Zynq-7020 module with a carrier card
that has an HDMI output through an ADV7511 transmitter, an HDMI input through an ADV7611 receiver,
and a connector for the same PYTHON-1300-C camera module whose register sequence we
use~\cite{avnet_mzevk,avnet_hdl}. In its reference designs, the video timing is generated in the
programmable logic, but the frames are read from memory buffers that Linux fills and selects, and
the timing reaches the ADV7511 as sync codes embedded in the pixel data rather than as a vertical
sync pin~\cite{avnet_hdl}; tying a frame's pattern to a camera trigger requires writing the video
timing, pattern generation, and trigger logic in the FPGA fabric, which is the design this paper
describes. The MicroZed's links to a host computer are also Gigabit Ethernet and USB~2.0, while
1280$\times$1024 images at 10 bits per pixel and \SI{120}{fps} require \SI{197}{MB/s}. A camera
module with a MIPI CSI-2 interface does not avoid these problems: the Zynq-7000 and Artix-7 have
no hardened MIPI receiver, so the receiver must be written in the fabric with an external analog
front end~\cite{xilinx_xapp894}, and the global-shutter modules that a DLP projector requires
have register settings that, like the PYTHON~1300's, are not public.

To interface the PYTHON~1300 to the FPGA, we designed a custom daughter card that mounts on top
of the Pt~V2, using the board schematics and the connector pin assignments that Alchitry
publishes for designing boards that join its stack~\cite{alchitry_pt_sch,alchitry_labs}. The card
carries the PYTHON~1300 (1280$\times$1024, global shutter, four LVDS data lanes at
\SI{720}{Mbps}, 10-bit pixels)~\cite{onsemi_python1300}, routes its LVDS lanes to an FPGA bank
powered at \SI{2.5}{V}, and sits under a C-mount lens in a printed enclosure. The sensor is held in an Andon Electronics 48-pin LCC socket rather than soldered to the card.
Because the monochrome and color versions of the PYTHON~1300 share the same 48-pin LCC package
and pinout~\cite{onsemi_python1300}, the socket lets either version be inserted; the measurements
in this paper use the monochrome NOIP1SN1300A. The footprint dimensions of the socket, which
Andon's catalog does not give, were taken from the LUPA300 sensor carrier of
Ruffner~\cite{ruffner2018thesis,ruffner_lupa300}, and we compared our decoupling against the
same carrier.

The sensor board also generates the sensor's supplies, and the Pt~V2 cannot supply them directly.
The PYTHON~1300 requires \SI{1.8}{V}, \SI{3.3}{V}, and a pixel supply of \SI{3.3}{V} held within
\SIrange{3.25}{3.35}{V}, and it must be powered up in the order \SI{1.8}{V}, \SI{3.3}{V}, pixel
supply, and down in the reverse order~\cite{onsemi_python1300}. The Pt~V2's \SI{3.3}{V} rail is
produced by an ADP5052 regulator whose feedback resistors set it to \SI{3.278}{V}, with a
tolerance of $\pm$\SI{1.5}{\percent}~\cite{alchitry_pt_sch,adi_adp5052}, so at its low limit
it falls below the pixel supply's window. We therefore boost that rail to \SI{4.45}{V} with a
TPS61023 and regulate it back down with two TPS7A2033 low-dropout regulators, one for the
\SI{3.3}{V} supply and one for the pixel supply; a TPS7A2018 produces \SI{1.8}{V} directly from
the Pt~V2's rail, and two TLV803S voltage supervisors enforce the power-up and power-down
order~\cite{ti_tps61023,ti_tps7a20,ti_tlv803s}.

Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows how the FPGA design connects these boards. The HDMI datapath is
adapted from~\cite{hamsternz}, and the SLI pattern design follows the controller of Yu
\emph{et~al.}~\cite{yu2019dualspie,yu2020dualprojector}. The following
subsections describe the video path, the pattern generator, the genlock that times the camera to
the projector, and the image and control path.

\subsection{Video path}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:edid} gives the procedure by which the BYOP camera detects the projector and
learns which video modes it accepts. A projector lists those modes in its extended display
identification data (EDID), a block of 128 or 256 bytes that a video source reads over the display
data channel (DDC), an I$^2$C bus in the HDMI cable. The Hd~V2 board does not connect the
projector's hot-plug detect pin to the FPGA, so the BYOP camera detects the projector by whether
its DDC answers. Every \SI{0.5}{\second}, the FPGA reads the projector's EDID; a read that is not acknowledged means that
no projector is attached. After a successful read, the FPGA builds the EDID that it will present
to a host computer, keeping only the projector's modes whose pixel clocks the receiver can
recover, and writes it into the idle half of a double-buffered memory, so that a host never reads
a partly built block.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Projector detection and EDID merging}
\label{alg:edid}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Loop \ every \SI{0.5}{\second}
  \State read EDID block~0 from the projector's DDC at address \texttt{0xA0}
  \If{the read is not acknowledged}
    \State $\mathit{present} \gets$ false \Comment{no projector}
    \State \textbf{continue}
  \EndIf
  \If{byte 126 of block~0 is non-zero} \State read block~1 (CEA-861 extension) \EndIf
  \State $\mathit{present} \gets$ true
  \State $M \gets$ projector's established and standard timings
  \State $M \gets \{m \in M : \text{pixel clock of } m \in [25, 90]\,\text{MHz}\}$
  \If{block~1 lists 1280$\times$720@60 (VIC~4)} \State add it to $M$ as a standard timing \EndIf
  \State $E \gets$ template EDID with timings $M$, a preferred detailed timing, range limits,
         and a name
  \State write a new serial number into $E$ and recompute its checksum
  \State write $E$ into the idle buffer; swap buffers
  \State $\mathit{changed} \gets$ ($E$, less serial and checksum, differs from the last build)
\EndLoop
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:hpd} then decides what the host sees and which source drives the projector.
The FPGA raises the hot-plug signal on its HDMI input only when a projector is present and a
merged EDID has been built, and whenever either condition changes, or the content of the merged
EDID changes, it holds the signal low for \SI{500}{\milli\second} before raising it again. A host
that sees the signal fall for longer than its debounce interval treats the display as unplugged
and, when the signal returns, reads the new EDID; this matters for projectors that present a
placeholder EDID while warming up and replace it with their real EDID seconds later. Each new
build carries a new serial number, which prevents the host from reusing a cached copy.

\begin{algorithm}[b]
\caption{Host hot-plug and source selection}
\label{alg:hpd}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Loop \ every clock cycle
  \State $\mathit{sink} \gets \mathit{present} \wedge (\text{a merged EDID has been built})$
  \If{$\mathit{sink}$ changed $\vee\ \mathit{changed}$}
    \State $\mathit{HPD} \gets 0$; $t \gets$ \SI{500}{\milli\second}
  \ElsIf{$\neg\mathit{sink}$} \State $\mathit{HPD} \gets 0$
  \ElsIf{$t > 0$} \State $\mathit{HPD} \gets 0$; decrement $t$
  \Else \State $\mathit{HPD} \gets 1$ \Comment{host reads the merged EDID}
  \EndIf
  \If{the input has a TMDS clock, its recovery MMCM is locked, and its symbols decode}
    \State drive the projector from the host's video \Comment{pass-through}
  \ElsIf{decoding has been lost for \SI{0.5}{\second}}
    \State drive the projector from the FPGA's own timing \Comment{offline}
  \EndIf
\EndLoop
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

These steps address flaws in the EDID handling of the controller of Yu
\emph{et~al.}~\cite{yu2019dualspie,yu2020dualprojector}, most of which follow from its hardware.
Their HDMI shield wires the hot-plug signal of its input permanently to the computer's
\SI{5}{V} supply, so the FPGA cannot tell the computer to read the EDID again~\cite{mojo_hdmi_sch},
and it connects the video lines to the FPGA without the \SI{50}{\ohm} termination to \SI{3.3}{V}
that an HDMI receiver presents; in our experience, computers with NVIDIA graphics cards did not
detect it as a display. Its EDID was a fixed list of three modes chosen for one projector, and the
projector's own EDID was never read. The Hd~V2 terminates every video line and lets the FPGA drive
the hot-plug signal through a level translator~\cite{alchitry_hd_sch}, which makes
Algorithm~\ref{alg:hpd} possible.

\subsection{Pattern generator}
\label{sec:pattern}
In pass-through, the receiver deserializes and decodes TMDS and selects the recovery clock band
from a measured input frequency, so the multiplier follows the source, and a high-bandwidth MMCM
tracks spread-spectrum GPU clocks. With no PC, the device drives the projector itself: a dedicated
MMCM is retuned at run time through its dynamic reconfiguration port and a timing generator is
loaded from a table of 14 modes, with the projector's EDID acting as a filter on that table, never
as a source of timings, and modes preferred by refresh rate and then by pixel count. The fastest
modes in the table are 1280$\times$1024@60 and 800$\times$600@120 (CVT reduced blanking,
\SI{73.27}{\mega\hertz}), and all results in this paper use the second.

The two modes produce the same kind of pixel stream, a pixel clock with blanking, horizontal sync,
vertical sync, and 8-bit red, green, and blue values, and the FPGA switches between them at a
single point. In pass-through, the decoded pixels cross from the receiver's clock into the
transmitter's clock through a small elastic buffer, since the two clocks have the same frequency
but reach their circuits through different clock buffers, with a phase offset that the design
does not control. Offline, a timing generator loaded with the selected mode produces the
blanking and sync signals from the dedicated MMCM. A clock multiplexer chooses the receiver's clock
only while the input has a clock, the receiver's MMCM is locked, and its symbols decode, and falls
back to the offline clock once decoding has been lost for \SI{0.5}{\second}; a registered
multiplexer selects the matching stream. Everything after that point, the pattern generator, the
lookup table, the TMDS encoder and serializer, and the vertical sync from which the camera's
trigger is timed, is the same circuit in both modes, so the patterns and the trigger timing do not
depend on where the video came from.

In either mode, the pattern generator replaces the active pixels with PMP patterns whose
parameters are derived from the resolution of the current video mode. The orientation of the
stripes is set by the user from the host over USB, as are the color channels, red, green, or
blue, that carry the pattern. Let $F$ be the number of active
pixels along the axis on which the patterns vary: the number of rows for horizontal stripes, or
the number of columns for vertical stripes. The generator sets
\begin{equation}
b = \left\lceil F/288 \right\rceil, \qquad P_k = \frac{288\,b}{m_k}, \quad m_k \in \{1, 6, 36\},
\label{eq:periods}
\end{equation}
so that the three periods $P_k$ are $288b$, $48b$, and $8b$ pixels, in the exact ratio
$1{:}6{:}36$, and the coarsest period spans the whole image. In our experience, about 36
periods across the field is the highest frequency that our scanners resolve reliably, and since
each frequency's phase is unwrapped using the next lower one, we want the scale factor between
successive frequencies to be as small as possible; with three frequencies reaching 36 periods,
equal steps of 6 minimize that factor. Every period is a multiple of 8, so
one eighth of a period, $P_k/8 = 36b$, $6b$, or $b$ pixels, is a whole number of pixels. For
800$\times$600 with horizontal stripes, $F=600$, $b=3$, and the periods are 864, 144, and 24 rows.

The value of pattern $n \in \{0,\ldots,7\}$ at frequency $k$ and position $y$, the row or column index along the varying axis, is
\begin{align}
I_{k,n}(y) &= L\!\left[\,c\big((\phi_k(y) - 512\,n) \bmod 4096\big)\right], \label{eq:pattern}\\
c(a) &= \left\lfloor 255\left(\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 \cos\tfrac{2\pi a}{4096}\right) + \tfrac12 \right\rfloor, \nonumber
\end{align}
where $c$ is a 4096-entry table of one cosine period, $L$ is a programmable 256-entry lookup
table that maps each 8-bit pattern level to the level sent to the projector, and $\phi_k(y)$ is the
table address of position $y$, produced by a phase accumulator that advances by
$m_k\lfloor 2^{24}/(288b) \rfloor$ per row or column and is read through its top 12 bits, so that it
advances by 4096 entries over one period $P_k$. Because $\phi_k(y)$ does not depend on $n$, and
$512$ is exactly one eighth of the 4096-entry table, pattern $n$ is pattern~0 shifted by exactly
one eighth of a period: the eight patterns read the same table entries in the same order, and
the rounding of the cosine and of the phase accumulator is identical in all eight, so it cancels
when the phase is recovered.

The lookup table $L$ is the point at which the BYOP camera compensates the HDMI signal for the
projector's electronics. It is the identity at power-up, so that the projector receives the
cosine levels unchanged, and the host can upload any 256-entry mapping over USB and read it back;
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} describes how the table is populated from
measurements of the projector. One sequence is the three frequencies times eight phases, plus flat
black and white frames for texture, which bypass $L$. For profiling, the same generator produces
impulse sequences, cycles of five full-field frames with programmable levels
(Section~\ref{sec:profiling}).

\subsection{Image path}
\label{sec:imagepath}
The FPGA configures the PYTHON~1300 over its serial peripheral interface (SPI), clocked at no
more than \SI{10}{\mega\hertz}, with a sequencer that walks the sensor's documented start-up
flow. After pulsing the sensor's reset and confirming its chip identifier, the sequencer uploads
the register sequence published by Avnet~\cite{avnet_hdl}: two stages of clock management with a
wait for the sensor's PLL to lock, the required upload of reserved registers, the soft power-up
that enables the LVDS drivers, and the sensor's 104-entry timing program, which sets how the
pixel array samples and transfers each exposure.

The sensor runs in its PLL mode, in which the FPGA supplies a \SI{72}{\mega\hertz} reference and
the sensor multiplies it by five, so that it sends data on four LVDS lanes and a fifth sync lane
at \SI{720}{Mbps}, together with a forwarded \SI{360}{\mega\hertz} clock. The FPGA receives the
forwarded clock through an I/O clock buffer and a regional buffer that divides it by five, and
deserializes each lane into 10-bit words at \SI{72}{\mega\hertz}. While idle, the sensor sends
the same training word on every lane, and the FPGA shifts the word boundary of each lane until
that word appears, which aligns all five lanes independently.

The sync lane carries codes that mark the start and end of each frame and line and say whether
the data lanes hold image pixels, black reference pixels, or a checksum. The data lanes deliver
the pixels in kernels of eight, two words per kernel, with even kernels in ascending and odd
kernels in descending order; the FPGA reorders each kernel and emits all eight pixels at once,
so that it keeps pace with the sensor's four pixels per word clock, 288 million pixels per second.
A full 1280$\times$1024 frame therefore arrives in \SI{4.55}{\milli\second}, faster than USB
can carry it, so each frame crosses into the clock of the DDR3 controller through a dual-clock
FIFO and is written into a four-slot ring of frames in DDR3 memory.

A second dual-clock FIFO carries the frames from DDR3 to the FT601 bridge on the Ft+~V2, whose
32-bit FIFO interface runs on a \SI{100}{\mega\hertz} clock that the bridge itself supplies. Each
frame is sent as a header followed by its pixels, packed at 10 bits, four pixels in five bytes.
The same USB link carries a command channel through which the host reads and writes the FPGA's
registers and uploads the lookup table of Section~\ref{sec:pattern}. \pending{Resource
utilization of the current bitstream.}

The header carries everything that the host would otherwise have to infer about the frame, so
that each frame describes itself. Its first 32 bytes begin with a marker word and end with the
marker's bitwise complement, so that a host that loses its place in the stream can find the next
frame, and between them hold the frame's rows and columns, its length in bytes, the pixel
format, and a frame index that increments with every frame sent, so that a gap in the index
reveals a lost frame. They also hold the number of frames that arrived short and were padded,
the value of the top-left pixel of the HDMI frame being transmitted when the frame began, and the
mean of a 16$\times$16 pixel region of interest (ROI) at the center of the sensor, computed by the
fabric from the frame's own pixels as they arrive, with the number of pixels averaged, a flag for
an ROI that fell on the sensor's black reference rows, and a bit that marks whether a mean was
paired with the frame at all. The profiling of Section~\ref{sec:profiling} reads only this mean,
so a sweep transfers no images.

When the host enables it, a second 32-byte block names the projected frame that the exposure
captured: the number of projected frames since a reference frame, the frequency and phase of the
pattern on screen in that projected frame, and flags for whether each value is known. The host sets the reference frame with a command that zeroes, on the same vertical sync,
both this count and the pattern generator's sequence, so that frame zero always shows pattern
zero. Section~\ref{sec:stacking} describes how the label is carried from the trigger that opened
the exposure to the frame's header. \pending{The label block is implemented in the FPGA design
and host software but not yet verified on hardware.}

\subsection{Triggering with a long delay}
\label{sec:stacking}
The camera is synchronized to the projector by the vertical sync that the FPGA \emph{sends}, so
the timing reference is exact by construction, whether the video comes from a host or from the
FPGA itself. A trigger fires a programmable delay $D$ after that edge, counted in
\SI{10}{\nano\second} ticks of a 24-bit time base, and the exposure $\tau$ is set in units of
\SI{0.375}{\micro\second}.

The controller of Yu \emph{et~al.}~\cite{yu2020dualprojector} triggers an external Allied Vision
or Basler camera through GPIO, and the camera, not the controller, applies the trigger delay. The
controller expects the camera to drive one of its output lines from its \texttt{FrameTriggerWait}
signal, active while the camera waits for a trigger and inactive during its trigger delay and its
exposure, and it reads that line as camera-ready. The
controller issues a trigger only at a vertical sync that follows a rising edge of camera-ready,
so the trigger of the next frame is withheld until the camera has finished its delay and its
exposure.

Whether a scanner can run at the projector's full frame rate depends on when the light of each
pattern reaches the camera relative to the vertical sync that sent it. A projector does not
display a frame when it receives it: its electronics hold the frame for an input lag before the
light leaves the lens, and the exposure that captures a pattern must wait for that light. To
compare the two triggering schemes, let pattern $k$ be sent with the vertical sync at time $kT$,
and let its exposure open at $kT+D$ and close at $kT+D+\tau$. If $D$ is shorter than $T$, each
exposure opens before the next pattern is sent, and the camera is ready again in time. If $D$
is longer than $T$, the exposure of pattern $k$ opens after pattern $k+1$ has already been sent,
and the trigger for pattern $k+1$ falls while the camera is still waiting out the delay of
pattern~$k$. The second case is the one that a commodity projector produces: for the ML750ST
profiled in Section~\ref{sec:profiling}, the delay that collects the most light from a frame is
about $1.06\,T$.

\subsubsection{Ready-gated triggering}
The controller of Yu \emph{et~al.}~\cite{yu2020dualprojector} connects to each external camera
through four GPIO lines: a trigger output, a camera-ready input, a mode input that selects
between pass-through and pattern generation, and an output that marks the first frame of the
pattern sequence. The controller counts the rising edges of the camera-ready input. At each
rising edge of the vertical sync, if a rising edge of camera-ready has arrived since the previous
vertical sync, the controller advances the pattern index and pulses the trigger output during the
vertical sync. If none has arrived, the controller holds the current pattern for another frame
and issues no trigger.

The trigger therefore leaves the controller at the vertical sync, and the delay $D$ is applied by
the camera, which reports ready again only after its delay and exposure have ended. A pattern is
consequently held on screen for
\begin{equation}
n_{\text{gated}} \;=\; \left\lceil \frac{D+\tau}{T} \right\rceil
\label{eq:oneshot}
\end{equation}
frames, and the fastest frame period is the trigger delay plus the exposure. For the ML750ST,
$(D+\tau)/T=1.81$, so $n_{\text{gated}}=2$: each pattern is displayed for two frames, and the
scan rate falls from \SI{120}{\hertz} to \SI{60}{\hertz}. In pass-through mode the same
handshake reaches the host, which waits for the camera to report ready before it plays the next
frame.

The camera's side of the handshake is set up by the host software. For an Allied Vision camera,
the software makes Line0 the trigger input, firing on a rising edge after a \SI{10}{\micro\second}
debounce, and drives Line3 from \texttt{FrameTriggerWait}, so that the controller sees the camera
become ready. Line1 is an output under software control that selects between pass-through and
pattern generation; the software sets it low and then high to reset the controller's pattern
counter and start the sequence, and low again when the scan ends. That reset is how the host
knows which pattern each captured frame holds: after it, the $n$th frame captured holds the $n$th
pattern of the sequence. Line2 is an input that reads
the controller's first-frame marker. The camera captures a fixed number of frames equal to the
length of the pattern sequence, with a trigger delay of \SI{17.167}{\milli\second}, one frame at
\SI{60}{\hertz} plus \SI{500}{\micro\second}. When several cameras are used, all of them take
the trigger, but only the first reports ready and controls the mode. A Basler camera is
configured the same way on different lines, with Line1 as the trigger input and Line4 driven by
\texttt{FrameTriggerWait}.

Because the controller counts rising edges of the ready line, a burst or a glitch on that line
queues extra advances, and the pattern sequence then runs ahead of the captured frames by a
fixed amount for the rest of the scan. Before integrating the sensor, we revised this logic so
that at most one advance is owed at a time and a second ready edge before the next vertical sync
is discarded, the first advance after the mode line rises requires a new low-to-high transition
of the ready line, and the ready line must hold its level for about \SI{135}{\micro\second}
before it is believed, since the camera cable is long and unshielded. These changes remove the
failure, but not the limit of~\eqref{eq:oneshot}.

\subsubsection{Queued triggering}
The BYOP camera drives the PYTHON~1300 sensor directly, and the FPGA sets both the exposure and
the delay, so there is no ready signal to wait for. The sensor operates in pipelined global
shutter mode, integrating the next frame while it reads out the current one, and the pattern
generator advances on every vertical sync. The FPGA keeps a free-running 24-bit counter of
\SI{10}{\nano\second} ticks. At each vertical sync it writes the absolute fire time, the counter
value at that sync plus $D$, into a 32-entry first-in first-out (FIFO) queue, whether or not
earlier fire times are still waiting.

The FPGA fires the trigger at the head of the queue as soon as the counter reaches or passes its
fire time, testing the difference of the two in modular 24-bit arithmetic, so a trigger cannot be
skipped by missing an exact match. The number of triggers outstanding is counted with each push
and pop resolved in the same clock cycle, and it is reported to the host, which uses it to pair
captured images with patterns: image $n$ holds pattern $n-\lfloor D/T\rfloor$. A full queue
increments an overflow counter rather than dropping a trigger silently.

With this queue, a trigger is never withheld, and the only requirement is that one exposure and
the sensor's readout gap $g$ fit within one frame period,
\begin{equation}
\tau + g \;\le\; T,
\label{eq:stacked}
\end{equation}
which the FPGA enforces by limiting the exposure to $T$ less \SI{90}{\micro\second}, an
\SI{80}{\micro\second} gap and a \SI{10}{\micro\second} margin. The delay itself is free: $D$
may exceed several frame periods. The modular comparison is valid for delays below $2^{23}$
ticks (\SI{83.9}{\milli\second}), at which point the queue holds $\lceil D/T\rceil=11$ triggers
at \SI{120}{\hertz}, within its 32 entries. For the ML750ST, $\lceil D/T\rceil=2$ triggers are
outstanding at a time, and the scan runs at the full \SI{120}{\hertz}.

Each of the four GPIO functions of the ready-gated design has an internal counterpart. The
trigger is a signal inside the FPGA from the queue to the sensor, and the ready line is not
needed, since the FPGA itself enforces~\eqref{eq:stacked}; it remains as a register that the host
can drive, so that the ready-gated pacing can still be tested without a camera. The selection
between pass-through and pattern generation is a register written by the host over USB.

The reset line and the first-frame marker are replaced by the frame label of
Section~\ref{sec:imagepath}. The host's reset command zeroes the FPGA's count of projected frames
and the pattern generator's sequence on the same vertical sync, and from then on every vertical
sync has a number. Each entry of the trigger queue carries the number of the vertical sync that
scheduled it, so the label leaves the queue with its own trigger and cannot drift from it. The
FPGA records the pattern on screen in each projected frame in a 16-entry ring indexed by that
number, and when the scheduled exposure ends, at the trigger time plus $\tau$, it combines the two
into the label. Because the next trigger cannot fire until one frame period later and $\tau$ is
less than $T$ by~\eqref{eq:stacked}, the most recent trigger at that instant is always the one
that opened this exposure. The label then travels with the frame's pixels into its header, so
the host reads which pattern each frame holds from the frame itself rather than from the order in
which frames arrive.

\section{Profiling the Projector}
\label{sec:profiling}

Before the BYOP camera can scan with a commodity projector, it must know when the light of
each video frame leaves the projector, so that the trigger delay $D$ and the exposure $\tau$
can be chosen to collect that light. To measure it, we built a profiling tool that uses the
BYOP camera itself as the instrument. The tool drives the projector from the FPGA's pattern
generator, sweeps a short exposure across the frame, and saves every sweep, together with the
chosen delay and exposure, in a single document. All of the measurements in this section come
from one such document, recorded with the Optoma ML750ST driven at 800$\times$600 and
\SI{120}{\hertz} with no host computer attached; the measured frame period is
$T=\SI{8325.81}{\micro\second}$.

For profiling, the sensor is mounted without a lens on the axis of the projection lens, about
\SI{17}{\milli\metre} in front of it in a printed cap, so that it receives light only when an LED
is on and the corresponding mirrors are in their on state. An iris on the cap keeps the brightest
reading below saturation, and filters can be placed in front of the sensor. Each reading is the
mean of the $16\times16$ pixel region of interest that the FPGA computes and returns in the frame
header, so a sweep transfers no images.

\subsection{Sweeping the exposure}
Let $E(t)$ be the light reaching the sensor at time $t$ after the vertical sync of frame~0. An
exposure of length $\tau$ opened at delay $d$ reads
\begin{equation}
r(d) \;=\; g\!\int_{d}^{d+\tau}\! E(t)\,dt \;+\; o \;+\; n,
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
with gain $g$, an offset $o$, and noise $n$. A sweep of $d$ in steps no longer than $\tau$
therefore samples $E$ filtered by a box of width $\tau$. The tool uses
$\tau=\SI{4.875}{\micro\second}$, a requested \SI{5}{\micro\second} exposure rounded to 13 of the sensor's
\SI{0.375}{\micro\second} exposure units, and steps of
\SI{5}{\micro\second}, for 1664 delays per frame. At each delay it discards four frames and
averages the next 20.

The pattern generator repeats a cycle of five full-field frames, and the tool records the reading
of every frame in the cycle, so a single sweep across one frame period yields a profile of all
five frames. The first sequence we swept, \emph{WWWWW}, shows white in every frame (255 in
frame~0 and 254 in frames~1--4, so that the frame header still marks the start of the cycle).
Its purpose was to see when the projector emits light. Figure~\ref{fig:timeline} shows the result
in blue: within each frame, the light arrives as a train of separate pulses, and the reading
never returns to the camera's dark level of 18.33~ADU (its minimum is 30.25~ADU), so with a
continuously lit picture there is no dark interval anywhere in the frame.

These observations rule out the usual approach to tone correction, in which the output of the
projector is measured against the level of the current frame alone. Because the light collected
during one frame's exposure also depends on the frame before it, the correction for the current
frame must instead be measured as a function of the level of the previous frame, and because the
effect differs from one LED to the next, it must be measured separately for red, green, and blue.

\subsection{Choosing the delay and the exposure}
\label{sec:exposure}
With the white frame's light identified, the delay and exposure follow from it. Let
$x(t)=\max(0,\,r_{\text{WKKKK}}(t)-\bar r_0)$. The share of one frame's light that an exposure
$[s,\,s+\tau]$ collects is
\begin{equation}
C(s,\tau) \;=\; \frac{\int_{s}^{s+\tau} x(t)\,dt}{\int_{0}^{5T} x(t)\,dt},
\label{eq:capture}
\end{equation}
and the tool places the exposure at the start $s$ that maximizes $C$ for the longest exposure the
sensor allows, after which the user can move either edge. The trigger delay is $D=s$. For the
ML750ST, the edges saved in the document are $s=\SI{8800.65}{\micro\second}$ and
$s+\tau=\SI{15057.48}{\micro\second}$, an exposure of \SI{6256.83}{\micro\second} that collects
\SI{95.6}{\percent} of the white frame's light. Since $D>T$, the exposure for each pattern opens
after the vertical sync of the next pattern, which is the case analyzed in
Section~\ref{sec:stacking}.

\subsection{The runway}
\label{sec:runway}
The WKKKK sweep was intended only to locate the white frame's light, but it showed something we
had not expected: the difference between the white frame and the black frames extends beyond one
frame period. Light from the white frame begins to arrive before the leading edge of the
exposure window, in an interval we call the \emph{runway}, $[\rho,\,s]$ with $\rho\ge s-T$. The
tool places $\rho$ by walking back from $s$ while $x(t)$ remains above \SI{2}{\percent} of its
peak, which for the ML750ST gives $\rho=\SI{5970.99}{\micro\second}$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:zoom}).

The runway overlaps the exposure of the previous pattern, which ended at
$s+\tau-T=\SI{6731.67}{\micro\second}$, and within that overlap lies a pulse of light from the
prior frame. The runway therefore divides into two parts,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal R_1=[\rho,\; s+\tau-T], \qquad \mathcal R_2=[s+\tau-T,\; s],
\end{equation}
the part inside the previous exposure, \SI{760.7}{\micro\second} long, and the part between
exposures, \SI{2069.0}{\micro\second} long. They hold \SI{0.35}{\percent} and \SI{3.65}{\percent}
of the white frame's light, respectively.

\subsection{Crosstalk maps}
\label{sec:crosstalk}
To find whose light arrives in each part of the runway, the pattern generator switches to a
two-frame cycle $A\,B\,A\,B$ of full-field levels. A reading in frame $k$ then sees that frame's
level $x$ and the level $y$ of frames $k\pm1$, which are equal. For each part $\mathcal R_i$, the
tool opens an exposure at the start of the part, lasting its length, and records 10 readings for
every pair $(A,B)$; each pair fills the two cells $(y,x)=(B,A)$ and $(A,B)$ of a $256\times256$
map. A map that varies with $y$ shows light from the neighboring patterns, and one that varies
with $x$ shows light from the runway's own frame. Because $A\,B\,A\,B$ makes the previous and the
next frame equal, the maps cannot tell those two apart.

The two maps, Figs.~\ref{fig:runway_prev} and~\ref{fig:runway_between}, behave differently. In $\mathcal R_1$, the reading is set by
the level $y$ of the neighboring frames and spans 24 to more than 1023~ADU as $y$ changes, but
not monotonically: it stays near 24~ADU up to $y\approx88$, rises in a band near $y=96$--120,
returns to the floor near $y=136$, and saturates from about $y=164$. In $\mathcal R_2$, the
reading is set instead by the runway frame's own level $x$, rising from 20.0 to 57.2~ADU across
$x$ at $y=0$, with a step of about 8~ADU between $y=240$ and $y=248$. Read with the one-frame
latency, $\mathcal R_1$ contains light of the previous pattern, which the previous exposure is
meant to collect and the current one must not, while $\mathcal R_2$ contains early light of the
pattern about to be captured. \pending{Interpret the non-monotonic dependence on $y$; test
whether the order of the prior frame's bit-planes explains it.}

Over most of their range, each map therefore depends on one level only. The map of
$\mathcal R_2$ is constant along $y$ for $y<248$, and the map of $\mathcal R_1$ is constant along
$x$ for $x<193$. Above that level, the reading in $\mathcal R_1$ rises by 5 to 8~ADU at every
value of $y$ that does not saturate the sensor, from 24.7 to 29.0~ADU at $y\le88$. This step is
the one place in either map where the reading depends on the level of a second frame, and it is
small against the light of the previous pattern in the same interval. On the edges of the bands
in $y$, near $y=96$, 128, and 144, the reading in $\mathcal R_1$ also varies by up to about
100~ADU across $x$.

\subsection{Color sweeps}
\label{sec:color}
The pulse that falls inside $\mathcal R_1$ raises the question of which LED emits it, and so we
repeated the WWWWW sweep through red, green, and blue filters on the camera. With $w(t)$ and
$f_c(t)$ the white and filtered sweeps less $\bar r_0$, the tool fits
\begin{equation}
w(t) \;\approx\; \sum_{c\in\{R,G,B\}} a_c\, f_c(t) + b
\label{eq:rgbfit}
\end{equation}
by least squares over every delay, where the weights $a_c$ absorb the transmission of each
filter. For the ML750ST the fit gives $a_R=0.4935$, $a_G=1.2192$, $a_B=0.6945$ and
$b=-3.28$~ADU, with a residual of 9.61~ADU RMS against a 771.7~ADU peak and $R^2=0.99880$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:rgb}). The white output of the projector is therefore the sum of its red, green,
and blue pulses, with no interval in which a fourth component, such as white from two LEDs at
once, contributes light that the three filtered sweeps do not account for. The largest residuals
sit on pulse edges, where a small timing difference between sweeps leaves a spike, and inside the
long red pulse. \pending{The red sweep may be partly clipped; repeat it with the iris closed one
step.}

The color sweeps also show that the red, green, and blue pulses occupy separate intervals of the
frame, and that the pulse inside $\mathcal R_1$, near the end of the frame, is blue
(Fig.~\ref{fig:rgb_zoom}). A scan made
through a red or green filter on the camera lens would therefore receive no light from the prior
frame inside its exposure, so that a target can be scanned without this crosstalk by choosing the
filter on the camera. The filter must be on the camera and not the projector's video: sending a
single primary over HDMI does not guarantee that only one LED emits light, since the projector's
color processing can drive other LEDs from a pure primary input (Section~\ref{sec:rw_electronics}),
whereas a filter on the camera lens passes only the light of one LED whatever the projector does.
The cost of the filter is the light of the other two LEDs, which lengthens the exposure.

These measurements give the delay and exposure for the scanner, and they identify where its
crosstalk comes from. Whether the scanner then measures phase correctly is the subject of the
next section.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
The profiles are ROI means over a 16$\times$16 patch with the sensor at the lens, not images of
a scene; a pixel in a scanned scene integrates the same pulse train through a different optical
path. The A\,B\,A\,B cycle confounds the
previous and the next pattern. One projector has been measured.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Putting the camera inside the HDMI path turns an unmodified projector into an instrument whose
timing can be measured rather than assumed. The same genlocked exposure that captures
structured light, stepped through an impulse sequence, recovers the projector's latency, its LED
pulse train, an exposure window that collects nearly all of a frame's light, and the light that
leaks into that window from neighbouring patterns. \pending{Summarize the 3D results.}
