Measurement report · 9 September 2026

ML750ST Off-Time Emission

The projector emits light in the gaps where no colour sub-field should be active — and how much depends on the picture level it was told to display. Measured with a genlocked PYTHON 1300 at 4.88 µs resolution.

Optoma ML750ST · single 0.45″ DMD, RGB LED frame 8325.8 µs (120.11 Hz) exposure 4.88 µs gain ×1.00

WHAT WAS BEING ASKED

A DMD steers light; it does not dim it

A digital micromirror device makes grey by tilting mirrors toward or away from the lens. The LED behind them is either lit or not. So on the simplest model, the light present between colour sub-fields — the leakage past mirrors in their off state — should be the same whatever picture level is commanded. Only the mirror duty changes.

Tonight's measurements say otherwise, and the departure is large.

gap8000_rgb.csv · 256 levels × 3 colours, delay held at 8000 µs

Light in the gap tracks the commanded level

The delay was pinned at 8000 µs — a point in the frame where none of the three colours is pulsing — and held there while the commanded level swept 0 to 255 for each LED in turn. A solid field, no sequence, nothing blinking.

At a delay where the projector should be dark, commanding a brighter picture makes more light come out. The LED drive follows the content — the mirrors are not the only thing being modulated.

THE PART THAT WASN'T EXPECTED

All three break at the same level

Red departs the floor at level 81, green at 83, blue at 79. Three LEDs of different materials, efficiencies and drive currents, sharing a threshold within four levels out of 256.

That common breakpoint is hard to explain as a property of the LEDs themselves. It looks like a decision made upstream of them — a drive scheme that keeps the emitter at a floor state until the requested picture crosses roughly a third of full scale, then begins scaling it. The ML750ST’s specified 20,000:1 full-on/full-off contrast is only reachable by modulating the light source with content, so a mechanism of this kind has to exist; what the measurement adds is where its knee sits.

Below the knee the projector is not simply dimmer. It is emitting a fixed amount, indistinguishable from black, no matter what it is asked for. That is why a commanded red of 16 was plainly visible to the eye as a flicker while reading −0.03 ADU against 0.11 of noise: the eye was integrating a whole frame of sub-field pulses, and the sensor’s 4.88 µs window at one delay was sampling a floor that never moved.

rgb_kkk_5us.csv · 1666 delays × 3 colours, against baseline_kkkkk_5us.csv

Integrated over the frame, the LEDs are closer than they look

Each colour was swept across the whole frame at 5 µs steps and its emission integrated against an all-black baseline measured on the same grid at the same exposure. The profile below is the raw material for that integral: 1666 delay points per frame, five frames laid end to end, 33,320 camera frames per colour with no dropped triggers.

Correction

Earlier in the session these three were compared at a single delay and reported as red being weaker than blue by more than ten to one. That was wrong. In a field-sequential projector a single-delay reading measures how much of that colour’s sub-field happens to overlap the sampling window, not how much light the LED produces.

Integrated across the frame the real ratio is 3.3 : 2.0 : 1.0 for blue : green : red. The sensor is not the cause of the imbalance either — PYTHON 1300 mono quantum efficiency runs about 50% at 450 nm, 60% at 550 nm and 57% at 630 nm, flat to within a factor of 1.2 across the visible.

baseline_kkkkk_5us.csv · 1666 delays, all frames black

The true black floor is small — and never reached

With every frame commanded black, the light across a whole frame period varies between 155.0 and 172.6 ADU: a swing of 17.6 counts on a 142-count pedestal. That is the genuine off-state leakage, and it is structured rather than flat, following the sub-field sequence.

Against that floor, a colour driven at full level never returns to it anywhere in the frame:

  • Blue never drops below 619 ADU — roughly 450 counts of light present at every delay.
  • Green never drops below 382 — about 210 counts continuous.
  • Red never drops below 191 — about 30 counts continuous.

So a full-brightness field is not a train of pulses against darkness. It is a raised continuous floor with pulses on top, and the height of that floor is what the level control is moving.

Resolution

The 1 µs step is finer than the measurement can actually resolve. Sweeping exposure against structure showed that 1.12, 4.88 and 20 µs all return identical detail while 400 µs visibly blurs it — so this sensor has an exposure floor between 20 and 400 µs, and XIMEA publish 29 µs as the PYTHON 1300 minimum.

With a window of roughly 25 µs, samples 1 µs apart overlap by about 96%. Both traces are therefore smoothed by the aperture rather than by the projector, and the finest step carrying new information is nearer 8 µs. Both sweeps sat at the same floor at the same commanded 1.12 µs, so they stay directly comparable to each other whatever that floor turns out to be. Every exposure quoted elsewhere in this report as 4.88 µs was in reality at that floor too; it is a uniform factor, so the ratios and timings above are unaffected while the absolute figure is not.

CONSEQUENCE

What this means for structured light

A binary-coded pattern sequence assumes a frame commanded black is dark. On this projector it is dark only while the whole picture stays below the knee. Raise the pattern’s bright level past roughly level 80 and the black frames stop being black — not because of crosstalk between frames, but because the light source itself is being held up by the content.

Two practical consequences follow. Coded sequences should either stay below the knee, where the floor is genuinely flat, or be calibrated against a baseline captured at the same average picture level rather than against an absolute black. And any per-frame measurement has to be paired with what was actually transmitted, since the projector’s response depends on the whole frame’s content and not only on the pixel being measured.

METHOD

Why these numbers are trustworthy

Every reading arrives paired, in hardware, with the pixel that produced it. The FPGA samples the first active pixel of each transmitted frame off its own output and ships it in the same 21-byte telemetry line as the ROI mean and a trigger ordinal:

R=mmm,nnn,rrggbb,cc<CR><LF>

  • The transmitted pixel is checked against the commanded level at every point; all 256 levels of the ramp were confirmed on the wire with zero mismatches.
  • The trigger ordinal increments once per trigger, so a lost frame is a visible gap rather than a silently closed-up curve. Zero gaps across 33,320-frame sweeps.
  • npx must read 256 — the count of pixels that landed in the 16×16 ROI. A drifted ROI is caught rather than averaged in. Zero failures.
  • The genlock delay is written, then polled until the FPGA’s own register confirms it, before any capture.

The camera side does one job: trigger on the projector’s vsync after a programmable delay, integrate for a programmable exposure, average the 16×16 patch at the sensor centre, and pair that number with the pixel that was on screen when the exposure opened. No frame buffer, no DDR3, no USB3, no fabric-side grouping.

What a running scan actually emits

Every profile above was taken with a solid field on the screen. A structured-light scan never projects one. These sweep the same frame period at the same 1 µs step, but with the eight phase-shifted fringes of the finest octet cycling one per frame — the stimulus a real scan uses — and average the eight at every delay by taking each pattern’s own mean, so an uneven number of frames per pattern cannot tilt a point.

The two charts differ in one thing only: which way the stripes run. That turns out to change what is being measured.

Neither set clips anywhere. Not one point of any sweep reaches 1023, where the solid-field profiles spent 9.7% (red) and 1.6% (blue) of the frame pinned to the ceiling. These are measurements end to end.

The floor reaches black — in blue too. Red under fringe content bottoms out at 152.1 ADU against an all-black baseline of 155, and blue at 161.9. Solid red never went below 203; solid blue never went below 721.6, the single most stubborn number in this report. Same projector, same 3D mode, same exposure — only the content differs, and blue’s floor falls by 560 ADU. The elevated floors in the solid-field profiles are a property of what is on the screen, not of an LED that cannot switch off.

The per-pattern spread is a detector. Where the eight patterns agree, the ROI is seeing the projector’s light and nothing else; where they diverge, the mirrors are delivering image content to it. Vertical keeps that number near 1.5 ADU for most of the frame, which is what makes “treat the eight as one field” a fair description rather than an approximation. Horizontal drives it to 543 and 740 at the 95th percentile, and its peaks fall to roughly 70% of the vertical ones — the average of eight patterns, half of them dark at the ROI, is not the emission.

STILL OPEN

What tonight did not settle

  • Is the knee a drive threshold or a duty change? Sweeping level at several delays at once would separate a source being turned up from a sub-field being widened.
  • Does the knee move with the projector’s own brightness and contrast settings? These runs were taken in Eco with both at zero. If the knee tracks those controls it is picture processing; if it stays put it is closer to the lamp driver.
  • The 7200 µs feature. An excursion at 86.5% of the frame survived a complete rewrite of the camera datapath and appeared with nothing projected. It moved with exposure across three settings, which points at the sensor’s readout colliding with the display’s frame boundary, but a confirming sweep saturated before it could resolve it.
  • Native resolution and refresh. Everything here ran at 800×600 or 640×480 into a panel that is natively 1280×800, and 120 Hz is documented as a 3D-only mode on this model. Repeating at 1280×800 @ 60 would remove both the scaler and the 3D path.
